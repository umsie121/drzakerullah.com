**In short:** for most women with early breast cancer, breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy) followed by radiotherapy gives the same long-term survival as mastectomy. The choice depends on the size and position of the cancer relative to the breast, whether there is more than one area, genetics and, above all, your own priorities.

This was the first question Rosie Fawehimi asked me on the [Everything Changed podcast](~/podcast/), and it is the question I discuss most often in clinic.

## The factors that decide

- **Tumour size relative to breast size.** A 3 cm cancer in a large breast can often be removed with a good result. In a small breast the same cancer may leave a significant deformity, unless [oncoplastic techniques](~/news/what-is-oncoplastic-breast-surgery/) are used.
- **More than one area.** Cancer in several separate parts of the breast may be better treated by mastectomy.
- **Genetics.** Women with a BRCA or other high-risk gene variant may choose mastectomy, sometimes on both sides, to reduce future risk.
- **Radiotherapy.** Breast conservation is almost always followed by radiotherapy. For a few women, avoiding radiotherapy matters a great deal.
- **Your priorities.** Some women want to keep their breast if at all possible. Others want the reassurance of mastectomy. Both are legitimate choices.

## Is mastectomy "safer"?

Not for most early breast cancers. Large long-term studies show equivalent survival for breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy and for mastectomy. Mastectomy reduces the chance of cancer coming back in the same breast. It does not, by itself, improve overall survival in these patients.

## Where oncoplastic surgery changes the answer

Oncoplastic surgery reshapes the breast during the cancer operation. That allows larger cancers to be removed while keeping a natural shape. In women with larger breasts, a [therapeutic mammoplasty](~/procedures/breast-cancer-surgery/) removes the cancer as part of a breast reduction. If mastectomy is right for you, [immediate reconstruction](~/procedures/breast-reconstruction/) can often be done in the same operation.

## What recovery looks like

Recovery is usually quicker after breast-conserving surgery. Most of my patients go home the same day or the next morning. They are back at desk work within one to two weeks, and driving once they can make an emergency stop comfortably. Radiotherapy then follows a few weeks later, usually as a short daily course over one to three weeks.

After mastectomy without reconstruction, most patients stay one night. Desk work typically resumes at two to four weeks. With immediate reconstruction, the stay is usually one to three nights, and full recovery takes longer: around six weeks before returning to the gym, and longer still after flap surgery.

In both cases there is a small risk of fluid collecting under the wound (seroma), infection or bleeding. If lymph nodes are removed from the armpit, there is also a risk of arm swelling (lymphoedema). I go through the risks that apply to your operation before you consent.

## How each option affects follow-up

After breast-conserving surgery you will have yearly mammograms of the treated breast, usually for at least five years, alongside routine checks of the other breast. After mastectomy, the remaining breast is still screened. A reconstructed breast is generally followed by clinical examination rather than mammography, though this varies.

Neither option changes the need for other treatments. Whether you need chemotherapy, hormone therapy or targeted drugs depends on the biology of the cancer, not on the operation chosen.

## Living with the decision

Many women tell me that what matters most is feeling that the decision was theirs. Taking a week to think, speaking to a breast care nurse or meeting someone who has had each operation can all help. For most early breast cancers, a short pause to be certain of the plan does not affect the outcome. If timing is critical in your case, I will tell you plainly.

It is also worth remembering that a decision is not always final. Women who have breast-conserving surgery can, if needed later, have a mastectomy. Women who have a mastectomy without reconstruction can have [delayed reconstruction](~/procedures/breast-reconstruction/) months or years afterwards, and there is no time limit.

## Questions to ask your surgeon

1. Am I a candidate for breast-conserving surgery? If not, why not?
2. What will my breast look like afterwards?
3. Will I need radiotherapy with either option?
4. If I choose mastectomy, can I have reconstruction at the same time?

If you are not getting clear answers, a [second opinion](~/second-opinion/) is always reasonable.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is survival better after mastectomy than lumpectomy?
For most early breast cancers, no. Breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy gives equivalent long-term survival.

### Do I need radiotherapy after a lumpectomy?
Usually, yes. Radiotherapy is standard after breast-conserving surgery, though some lower-risk older patients may safely omit it after discussion with their team.

### Can I have reconstruction at the same time as a mastectomy?
Often, yes. Immediate reconstruction is possible for many patients and is planned before surgery.
