**In short:** most fibroadenomas can safely be left alone once triple assessment has confirmed the diagnosis. They are benign and do not usually raise your risk of breast cancer. Fibroadenoma removal is worth considering if the lump is large, growing, painful, uncertain on biopsy, or simply troubling you. It can be done by surgical excision or, for smaller lumps, vacuum-assisted excision.

A fibroadenoma is a smooth, rubbery lump made of gland and connective tissue. It is one of the most common breast lumps I see, particularly in women in their teens, twenties and thirties, though it can be found at any age. It often moves easily under the fingers, which is why it is sometimes called a "breast mouse".

The question I am asked most, once the diagnosis is clear, is whether it needs to come out. For many women the honest answer is no. But there are good reasons to remove some, and it is a decision you are entitled to share in.

## First, be sure it is a fibroadenoma

Before anyone talks about leaving a lump alone, the diagnosis has to be secure. A fibroadenoma cannot be diagnosed by feel alone, however typical it seems. In a UK breast clinic that means [triple assessment](~/procedures/benign-breast-conditions/):

1. **Examination** by a breast specialist.
2. **Imaging**, usually ultrasound in younger women, with a mammogram added depending on your age and findings.
3. **A core needle biopsy** if there is any doubt, taken under local anaesthetic.

In some younger women with a lump that looks entirely typical on examination and ultrasound, a biopsy is not always needed. From the age of around 25, or whenever anything looks less than classic, most breast teams will take a biopsy so the diagnosis is confirmed under the microscope. When all three parts of the assessment agree, you can be very confident in the answer.

## When leaving it alone is the right choice

Once a fibroadenoma has been confirmed, monitoring is a perfectly reasonable choice for most women. Fibroadenomas behave in one of three ways over time:

- **Most stay much the same size** for years.
- **Some shrink,** and many become smaller or harder after the menopause.
- **A minority grow,** sometimes during pregnancy or in the teenage years.

A simple fibroadenoma does not turn into cancer, and having one does not usually change your breast cancer risk. So if the lump is small, does not bother you and has been confirmed by biopsy or typical imaging, there is no medical need to remove it.

What I tell patients is to get to know how it feels, and to come back if it changes. A lump that is clearly getting bigger, a new lump elsewhere in the breast, or any skin or nipple change should be reassessed. Some clinics arrange a repeat ultrasound after a few months; others simply ask you to return if you notice a change. Either is sensible, and your team will tell you which they recommend.

## Reasons for fibroadenoma removal

There are four main situations in which I would discuss removing a fibroadenoma.

- **Size.** A larger fibroadenoma can be visible, change the shape of the breast or feel uncomfortable. Removal is often more straightforward while it is still a moderate size.
- **Growth.** A lump that keeps getting bigger should be looked at again, and removal is usually the clearest way to settle the question.
- **Uncertainty.** Occasionally the biopsy does not give a clear answer, or the features suggest something other than a simple fibroadenoma. In that case removing the whole lump lets the pathologist examine all of it.
- **Your preference.** Some women find that living with a lump, even a benign one, causes ongoing worry, or makes it harder to examine their own breasts. That is a legitimate reason to have it removed, and I take it seriously.

Pain alone is a less reliable reason. Fibroadenomas are not usually painful, and if a lump is tender the discomfort is sometimes coming from the surrounding breast tissue rather than the fibroadenoma itself. Removing it does not always cure breast pain, and I would rather say that before surgery than after.

## A word about phyllodes tumours

Phyllodes tumours are rare. They can look very similar to a fibroadenoma on a scan and even on a needle biopsy, but they tend to grow more quickly. Most are benign, a smaller number are borderline and only a few are malignant.

The reason they matter here is that they are treated differently. A phyllodes tumour needs to be removed with a margin of normal tissue around it, because it can come back in the same place if it is not fully cleared. So a "fibroadenoma" that is growing quickly, or one where the biopsy raises the possibility of phyllodes, should be removed surgically rather than watched or taken out by vacuum. This is one of the main reasons I prefer a lump that is changing to be reassessed rather than left.

## Surgical excision or vacuum-assisted excision?

There are two ways to remove a fibroadenoma, and the right one depends mainly on its size, its position and what the biopsy has shown.

### Surgical excision

This is the traditional operation. I make a small incision, usually placed around the edge of the areola or in a natural skin crease so the scar is as discreet as possible, and remove the lump whole. It is usually done as a day case, most often under general anaesthetic, though a small lump near the surface can sometimes be removed under local anaesthetic.

Surgical excision is the better choice for larger fibroadenomas, for anything where phyllodes is a possibility, and where the biopsy result is uncertain. The whole lump comes out in one piece and is sent to the laboratory, which gives the most complete answer.

### Vacuum-assisted excision

This is a newer, less invasive option for smaller lumps. Under local anaesthetic and ultrasound guidance, a needle connected to a gentle suction device is passed through a cut of a few millimetres. The fibroadenoma is removed in small pieces through the same needle. It is usually carried out by a breast radiologist, there are no stitches, and you go home shortly afterwards.

NICE has reviewed vacuum-assisted excision of benign breast lesions (interventional procedures guidance IPG156) and considers it safe and effective enough for routine use with the usual consent and audit arrangements. Its limits are worth knowing:

- It suits **smaller fibroadenomas**. Larger lumps are less likely to be removed completely.
- Because the tissue comes out in fragments, the laboratory sees the lump in pieces rather than whole.
- A small part can occasionally be left behind or grow back, and some women go on to need surgery.

Availability varies between hospitals and insurers, so I will tell you honestly whether it is a realistic option for your lump.

## What recovery looks like

Recovery from either procedure is usually quick.

- **After vacuum-assisted excision,** expect some bruising and tenderness for a few days. A firm dressing or supportive bra helps. Most women return to normal activities within a day or two, avoiding strenuous exercise for a short while.
- **After surgical excision,** most women take a few days to a week off work, depending on the job. Wear a supportive bra, including at night, for the first week or so. Dissolving stitches are usually used, and a wound check is arranged.

The main risks of either procedure are bruising, a collection of blood (haematoma), infection and, after larger lumps, a small change in the contour of the breast. These are uncommon, and I will go through the ones that apply to you before you decide. The removed tissue is always sent to the laboratory, and I see you to discuss the result.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do all fibroadenomas need to be removed?
No. Most can be left alone once triple assessment has confirmed the diagnosis. Removal is considered if the lump is large, growing, uncertain on biopsy or worrying you.

### Can a fibroadenoma turn into cancer?
A simple fibroadenoma does not turn into cancer and does not usually raise your breast cancer risk. Any lump that changes should still be reassessed.

### Can a fibroadenoma come back after removal?
The same lump rarely returns after complete surgical excision, though new fibroadenomas can develop elsewhere in the breast. Regrowth is a little more likely after vacuum-assisted excision, particularly of larger lumps.

### Will removal leave a scar?
Surgical excision leaves a small scar, usually placed at the edge of the areola or in a skin crease. Vacuum-assisted excision leaves a mark of a few millimetres that usually fades well.
