**In short:** a therapeutic mammoplasty removes a breast cancer using the techniques of a breast reduction. The cancer comes out with a margin of healthy tissue, and the remaining breast is reshaped into a smaller, lifted form. It usually suits women with larger or heavier breasts, and the other breast can be reduced to match.

When I explain this operation in clinic, I often see a moment of surprise. Many women arrive expecting a choice between a lumpectomy that may leave a dent and a mastectomy. For the right person, a therapeutic mammoplasty offers a third path: the cancer is treated, the breast is kept, and its shape is planned from the start.

It is one of the core techniques of [oncoplastic breast surgery](~/news/what-is-oncoplastic-breast-surgery/), which means removing the cancer completely and using plastic surgical methods to keep or restore the shape of the breast at the same operation.

## What is a therapeutic mammoplasty?

In a standard breast reduction, the surgeon removes a wedge of tissue and skin, lifts the breast and moves the nipple higher. The pattern of what is removed is chosen to leave a well shaped, smaller breast.

In a therapeutic mammoplasty, I plan that pattern so the cancer sits inside the tissue that is being removed. The cancer, with a rim of normal tissue around it, is taken out as part of the reduction. What is left is then reshaped. The nipple and the dark skin around it (the areola) are usually kept.

Because a reduction naturally removes a larger volume than a simple lumpectomy, I can take a wider margin or remove a larger cancer and still leave a natural looking breast. Cancer Research UK describes it as an option for women who need a large amount of tissue removed, where a standard operation would change the look of the breast.

## Who is it suitable for?

The decision rests on the cancer, the breast and, above all, on you. In general, a therapeutic mammoplasty is worth discussing when:

- **the breasts are medium to large, or have dropped with time.** There needs to be enough tissue to reshape.
- **the cancer is in one area of the breast,** rather than in several separate places.
- **the cancer would take a large share of the breast** with a standard lumpectomy, and so would leave a visible dent or distortion.
- **you would welcome smaller breasts.** Some women have lived with neck, back or shoulder pain from heavy breasts for years. For them, a lighter breast can feel like a positive outcome of a difficult time.
- **you can have radiotherapy afterwards,** which is part of almost every breast conserving treatment.

It is less suitable if your breasts are small, if you would prefer not to change size, or if the cancer is spread through the breast. For smaller breasts there are other ways of filling the space, such as moving nearby tissue into it, which I describe on my [breast reconstruction page](~/procedures/breast-reconstruction/). Smoking and poorly controlled diabetes raise the risk of wound problems, and I would talk about this openly before recommending any reshaping operation.

Every plan is also discussed by the multidisciplinary team, the radiologists, pathologists, oncologists and specialist breast nurses who agree treatment together.

## How the operation is planned

Planning is where most of the work happens. Before surgery I look at your scans and biopsy results and examine you sitting up, because the shape of the breast changes when you lie down.

1. **Locating the cancer.** If the cancer cannot be felt, a small marker is placed in it beforehand under ultrasound or mammogram guidance, so I can find it precisely in theatre.
2. **Choosing the pattern.** The position of the cancer decides which part of the breast is removed and which part is used to rebuild it. A cancer in the lower breast suits one design, one in the upper or outer breast suits another.
3. **The armpit.** Most patients also have a [sentinel lymph node biopsy](~/procedures/breast-cancer-surgery/), where the first one to three lymph nodes draining the breast are removed through a separate small cut and checked for cancer.

During the operation I usually place small metal clips where the cancer was. Because the tissue is moved around during reshaping, these clips show the radiotherapy team exactly where to aim if an extra dose to that area (a boost) is needed.

## Matching the other breast

Once one breast has been reduced, the other will be larger. Most women want them to match. There are two ways to do this:

- **At the same operation.** I reduce the other breast during the cancer surgery. You wake with two breasts of similar size and only one recovery. The treated breast may change a little with radiotherapy, so perfect symmetry at this stage cannot be promised.
- **Later.** Some women prefer to wait until radiotherapy has finished and the treated breast has settled, then have a [breast reduction](~/procedures/breast-reduction/) on the other side to match.

We talk it through, and the choice is yours. Surgery to the other breast for symmetry after cancer is usually covered by UK private medical insurers as part of cancer treatment, but please confirm with your insurer first. ## Recovery, scars and risks

The operation is done under general anaesthetic. Recovery is broadly similar to a breast reduction, and depends on whether one or both breasts are operated on and what is done in the armpit. Many women go home the same day or after one night. I give you a supportive bra to wear day and night for several weeks. Most return to desk work within one to two weeks and to full exercise at around six weeks, although this varies. My post on [recovery after a lumpectomy](~/news/lumpectomy-recovery/) covers the practical side of the first few weeks.

The scars follow a reduction pattern: usually around the areola, a vertical line down to the fold beneath the breast, and sometimes a line in the fold itself. They are red and firm at first and fade considerably over one to two years.

The risks I discuss before you consent include:

- bleeding, infection and fluid collecting under the wound (seroma);
- slow healing where the incisions meet, which is more likely in smokers;
- firm areas of fat that has lost its blood supply (fat necrosis), which can feel like a lump and sometimes needs a scan to confirm what it is;
- changes in nipple sensation, and an effect on breastfeeding;
- in a minority, cancer cells at the edge of the removed tissue, which means further surgery.

That last point deserves a word. The pathologist examines the edges (margins) of the removed tissue. If cancer cells reach an edge, more surgery is needed. After a reshaping operation, taking a little more tissue from the exact spot can be harder, and occasionally a mastectomy is the safer option. The wider margins a reduction allows make this less likely, and I explain how it would be handled before we begin.

Radiotherapy to the breast is usually started a few weeks after surgery, once the wounds have healed.

## How it compares with other options

A therapeutic mammoplasty is still breast conserving surgery. For most early breast cancers, breast conserving surgery followed by radiotherapy gives the same long-term survival as mastectomy. I have written more about that choice in [lumpectomy or mastectomy](~/news/lumpectomy-vs-mastectomy/).

What changes is the shape of the result. Compared with a simple lumpectomy, the breast is smaller and higher, with longer scars. Compared with a mastectomy and reconstruction, you keep your own breast, nipple and much of your sensation, usually with a shorter recovery.

If you have been told a mastectomy is your only option and you have larger breasts, it is reasonable to ask whether a therapeutic mammoplasty was considered. A [second opinion](~/second-opinion/) can help you feel sure of the plan. Sometimes mastectomy really is the better choice, and I will say so plainly.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a therapeutic mammoplasty the same as a breast reduction?
It uses the same techniques, but the purpose is different. The main aim is to remove the cancer with clear margins, and the reduction is planned around it so the breast keeps a good shape.

### Will I need radiotherapy after a therapeutic mammoplasty?
Usually, yes. It is a form of breast conserving surgery, and radiotherapy to the breast normally follows. Your oncologist will confirm what is right for you.

### Can both breasts be done at the same time?
Often, yes. The other breast can be reduced at the same operation, or later once radiotherapy has finished. We decide together which suits you.

### Is therapeutic mammoplasty covered by insurance?
It is part of breast cancer treatment, so it is usually covered, and surgery to the other breast for symmetry normally is too. Always check with your insurer and get pre-authorisation first.
