**In short:** most nipple discharge has a benign cause. Milky discharge from both breasts is usually hormonal or linked to medicines. Thick green or brown discharge is often duct ectasia. Clear or bloody discharge from one duct, appearing on its own, is most often a small benign growth called a papilloma, but it always needs a specialist check.

Nipple discharge frightens people more than almost any other breast symptom, and I understand why. Seeing a stain on your bra, especially if it looks like blood, is alarming. In clinic, though, the cause usually turns out to be benign. What matters is not so much the colour as a handful of other features, and those are what I look at first.

## What counts as normal

The breast is made of milk ducts that open on to the nipple. Even when you are not pregnant or breastfeeding, those ducts make small amounts of fluid. If you squeeze the nipple firmly, many women can express a drop or two from several ducts on both sides. That is normal, and it is one reason I ask patients not to keep squeezing to check. Repeated squeezing stimulates the ducts and can keep the discharge going.

Discharge is more likely to need assessment when it:

- comes out on its own, without squeezing, enough to mark clothing
- comes from one breast only
- comes from a single opening on the nipple rather than several
- is clear and watery, or contains blood
- comes with a lump, or with a nipple that has newly turned in, or a rash on the nipple
- occurs in a man

## The common causes of nipple discharge

### Milky discharge from both breasts (galactorrhoea)

Milky discharge outside pregnancy and breastfeeding is called galactorrhoea. It is not a breast disease. It happens when the hormone prolactin, which drives milk production, is raised or the breast is unusually sensitive to it. The common causes are:

- **Recent pregnancy or breastfeeding.** Small amounts of milk can carry on for many months after you stop.
- **Medicines.** Some antipsychotics, some antidepressants and some anti-sickness drugs raise prolactin, as can certain blood pressure medicines and opioids.
- **An underactive thyroid,** which is picked up on a simple blood test.
- **Frequent nipple stimulation,** including repeated checking.
- **A prolactinoma,** a benign growth in the pituitary gland at the base of the brain. This is uncommon. Clues include irregular or absent periods, headaches or changes in vision.

The first step is usually a blood test for prolactin and thyroid function, and a pregnancy test where relevant. Please do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own. Speak to the doctor who prescribed it.

### Thick green, yellow or brown discharge (duct ectasia)

As the breast ages, the ducts behind the nipple can widen and fill with thick fluid. This is duct ectasia. It is very common around and after the menopause, often comes from several ducts, and may be green, yellow, brown or almost black. It can also pull the nipple inwards in a characteristic slit-like way. Duct ectasia does not increase the risk of breast cancer, and most women need no treatment.

A related condition, periductal mastitis, is inflammation around the ducts. It is more common in smokers and can cause pain, redness, discharge with pus and sometimes an abscess. It needs treatment, and stopping smoking genuinely helps.

### Clear or bloody discharge from one duct (intraductal papilloma)

When a patient tells me the discharge comes from one spot on one nipple, appears on its own, and is clear or blood-stained, the most likely cause is an intraductal papilloma. This is a small, benign, wart-like growth inside a duct, usually close to the nipple. It is most common in women over 40. As [Breast Cancer Now explains](https://breastcancernow.org/about-breast-cancer/breast-lumps-and-benign-not-cancer-breast-conditions/intraductal-papilloma), a single papilloma does not raise your risk of breast cancer.

Less often, discharge like this is caused by ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), an early, non-invasive change in the cells lining the ducts, or by breast cancer. That is a minority of cases, but it is why this pattern always needs full assessment rather than a wait-and-see approach.

### Other causes

Infection (mastitis), a blocked duct and, during late pregnancy or early breastfeeding, a little blood-stained discharge from the ducts can all cause discharge. The last of these is usually harmless and settles, but it is still worth mentioning to your midwife or GP.

## When should you see a doctor?

The NHS advice is simple: see your GP about any [nipple discharge](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/nipple-discharge/). Most of the time the answer will be reassuring.

Under NICE guidance, GPs refer people aged 50 and over with discharge, retraction (the nipple pulling in) or other concerning changes in one nipple only on the urgent suspected cancer pathway, with an appointment within two weeks. Younger patients with spontaneous, one-sided or blood-stained discharge are usually referred to a breast clinic too, and so is any man with nipple discharge. If you are not sure how urgent your symptom is, I have written about [when a breast symptom needs a specialist](~/news/breast-lump-when-to-see-a-specialist/), and about the [two-week wait and private breast clinics](~/news/two-week-wait-vs-private-breast-clinic/).

:::note
A new rash, crusting or scaling on the nipple that does not heal, with or without discharge, should be checked promptly. Most are eczema, but a rare condition called Paget's disease of the nipple can look similar.
:::

## How I assess nipple discharge in clinic

Assessment follows the same triple assessment as any other breast symptom, adapted to discharge. You can read more about the approach on my [breast lumps and benign breast conditions](~/procedures/benign-breast-conditions/) page.

1. **History.** Which side, how often, whether it comes on its own, its colour, your medicines, and any recent pregnancy.
2. **Examination.** I examine both breasts and armpits and gently look for which duct, or ducts, the discharge comes from. That single detail tells me a great deal.
3. **A strip test.** A drop of discharge on a test strip shows whether it contains blood, even when it does not look bloody.
4. **Imaging.** An ultrasound behind the nipple, and a mammogram if you are around 40 or over. A papilloma can sometimes be seen on ultrasound.
5. **Biopsy, if anything is seen.** Usually a needle biopsy under local anaesthetic.

Occasionally, if the discharge is persistent and the tests are normal, an MRI scan helps.

## Treatment

If the tests are reassuring and the discharge is milky or from several ducts, no surgery is needed. Treating the cause, or simply not squeezing, is often enough.

When a single duct keeps producing clear or bloody discharge, or when a papilloma is found, I usually recommend removing it. The options are:

- **Vacuum-assisted excision,** which removes a small papilloma through a needle under local anaesthetic, without a cut on the breast.
- **Microdochectomy,** removal of the single affected duct through a small cut at the edge of the areola. It is usually a day case under general anaesthetic, and breastfeeding from that breast may still be possible.
- **Total duct excision,** removal of all the main ducts behind the nipple, for troublesome discharge from several ducts. Breastfeeding from that side is not possible afterwards, so I discuss this carefully with younger women.

Whichever is done, the tissue is examined under the microscope. That gives a definite diagnosis, which is often the most reassuring part of the whole process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is nipple discharge a sign of breast cancer?
Usually not. Most nipple discharge has a benign cause. Discharge that is bloody or clear, from one duct, and appears on its own is the pattern that needs prompt assessment.

### What causes milky nipple discharge when I am not pregnant?
Usually raised prolactin, often from recent breastfeeding, medicines or an underactive thyroid, and occasionally a benign pituitary growth. A blood test is the first step.

### Should I squeeze my nipple to check for discharge?
No. Most women can express a little fluid by squeezing, and repeated squeezing can keep discharge going. What matters is discharge that appears on its own.

### Can men get nipple discharge?
Yes, but it is uncommon in men and should always be checked by a breast specialist.
