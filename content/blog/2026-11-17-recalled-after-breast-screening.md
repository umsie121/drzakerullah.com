**In short:** being recalled after a mammogram means the screening team wants a closer look at an area on your X-ray, or the pictures were not clear enough. It does not mean cancer has been found. Most women who are recalled are told everything is normal. The assessment clinic usually involves more mammograms, an ultrasound and sometimes a needle biopsy.

A recall letter tends to arrive on an ordinary morning and change the whole day. In clinic I meet many women who have spent days imagining the worst after one. I understand why. The letter is short, the appointment can feel a long way off, and the word "recall" sounds alarming.

So I want to explain, as plainly as I can, what a recall actually means, what happens at the assessment clinic, and how to think about the result. I work in breast surgery in the NHS and privately, and the assessment process I describe here is the same careful approach we use for any breast change.

## Why women are recalled after a mammogram

NHS breast screening invites women from the age of 50 up to their 71st birthday, every three years. Two X-ray pictures are taken of each breast, and they are read by specialists who compare them with any earlier screening films. If something looks different, or cannot be seen clearly, you are asked back. There are two main kinds of recall.

### A technical recall

Sometimes the pictures simply are not good enough to read. The breast may not have been fully in view, or the image may be blurred. You are invited back for a repeat mammogram. A technical recall says nothing about your breast tissue, only about the picture.

### A recall for assessment

This is the more common worry. The readers have seen something they want to examine more closely. That might be:

- **An area of density or a shadow**, which often turns out to be normal breast tissue overlapping on the X-ray.
- **A small lump or mass**, which may be a cyst (a fluid-filled sac) or a fibroadenoma (a common benign lump made of gland and fibrous tissue).
- **Calcifications**, tiny specks of calcium. These are very common and are usually harmless. Certain patterns are checked more carefully because they can sometimes be an early sign of change in the milk ducts.
- **Distortion**, where the normal lines of the breast tissue look pulled in a slightly different direction. This can be caused by old scarring, or by tissue that simply sits in an unusual pattern.

You may also be recalled if you mentioned a breast symptom to the radiographer on the day, even if your mammogram looked normal.

## What happens at the breast assessment clinic

Your letter will give you an appointment at the screening service's assessment clinic and a number to call. If you have questions before then, ring and ask to speak to a breast care nurse. They are there to help, and many women find one phone call takes the edge off the wait.

At the clinic, the team aims to do all the tests you need in one visit. It can take a few hours, so it is worth clearing your diary. The tests may include:

1. **A conversation and examination.** A doctor or specialist practitioner asks about your health and family history and examines both breasts and armpits.
2. **More mammograms.** These are often magnified views, or views from a different angle, aimed at the area in question. Many apparent shadows disappear on these extra pictures.
3. **An ultrasound scan.** This uses sound waves, not radiation, and is very good at telling a cyst from a solid lump.
4. **A needle biopsy, if needed.** A small sample of tissue is taken under local anaesthetic, usually guided by ultrasound or by mammogram. If the area is only visible as calcifications, the biopsy is done with the breast held in the mammogram machine so the needle can be guided precisely.

These three parts together, examination, imaging and biopsy where needed, are known as triple assessment. It is the UK standard for checking any breast change, and it is how I assess [breast lumps, cysts and other benign breast conditions](~/procedures/benign-breast-conditions/) in my own clinics.

**What to bring:** your letter, a list of your medicines (especially any blood thinners), and someone to keep you company if you would like. Avoid deodorant or talcum powder on the day, as these can show up on a mammogram.

## How results are given

If the extra pictures and ultrasound are reassuring, you will often be told on the same day that all is well and that you will return to routine screening.

If you have a biopsy, the sample has to be examined under a microscope by a pathologist, so you will be given a separate appointment for the result, usually within a week or two. I know this second wait is hard. It does not mean the team suspects cancer. A biopsy is often the only way to be completely certain that something is benign, and in most cases that is exactly what it shows.

You may see your results described with a letter and a number. UK breast teams often score mammograms (M), ultrasound (U) and biopsies (B) on a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 is normal, 2 is benign and higher numbers mean more checking is needed. Your team should explain your own scores to you. If they do not, ask.

## If something is found

A minority of women who are recalled are diagnosed with breast cancer. If that is you, it helps to know that cancers found through screening are often small and may not yet be possible to feel. That is the whole purpose of the programme. Some women are found to have ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), an early change inside the milk ducts that has not spread into the surrounding breast tissue.

Finding cancer early generally means more treatment choices, and it makes it more likely that the breast can be kept. Your case will be discussed by a multidisciplinary team of surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists and breast care nurses before a plan is agreed. If surgery is advised, I have written about [how the choice between lumpectomy and mastectomy is made](~/news/lumpectomy-vs-mastectomy/), and there is more on [breast cancer surgery](~/procedures/breast-cancer-surgery/) itself.

You are entitled to ask questions, to take time to think and, if you wish, to seek a [second opinion](~/second-opinion/) before treatment begins.

## Should you go private after a recall?

For most women, the best place to be assessed after a recall is the NHS screening unit that called you back. That team has your screening films and earlier mammograms to compare against, and the assessment clinic is set up for exactly this purpose. I would not encourage anyone to leave that pathway simply out of worry.

Some women do choose to see me privately, for example if they would like a second look at results they have already been given, a longer conversation about what a benign finding means, or advice on their personal risk if breast cancer runs in the family. If that would help you, it is worth bringing copies of your reports. More about [booking a private consultation](~/booking/) is on my website.

:::note
Screening is for women without symptoms. If you notice a new lump, a change in breast shape, skin dimpling or a change in the nipple at any time, see your GP straight away, even if you have recently had a normal mammogram. The NHS explains [what your breast screening results mean](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/breast-screening-mammogram/your-results/) in more detail.
:::

## Frequently asked questions

### Does being recalled after a mammogram mean I have cancer?
No. Most women recalled after breast screening are found to have normal breast tissue or a benign change. A recall means the team wants a closer look to be certain.

### How long does a breast screening recall appointment take?
Allow a few hours. The team tries to complete all the tests you need, such as extra mammograms, an ultrasound and sometimes a biopsy, in a single visit.

### How long do biopsy results take after a screening recall?
The sample is examined by a pathologist, so results are usually given at a separate appointment, often within a week or two. Your clinic will tell you when to expect them.

### Will I go back to normal screening afterwards?
If your tests are normal or show a benign change that needs no follow-up, you return to routine screening every three years. Occasionally the team suggests an earlier check, and they will explain why.
