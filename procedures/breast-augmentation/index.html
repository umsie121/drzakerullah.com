Breast augmentation

Augmentation uses an implant to increase the size or restore the fullness of the breast. Mr Ullah brings an oncoplastic surgeon's perspective: the same operation he uses to rebuild symmetry after cancer, applied with the same care to cosmetic goals.

Written and medically reviewed by Mr Md Zaker Ullah FRCS, Consultant Oncoplastic Breast Surgeon · Updated 3 November 2026

An honest consultation

A good augmentation begins with an honest conversation about proportion — what an implant can and cannot do for your frame, how size, profile and placement (over or under the muscle) change the result, and how breast and implant will change over years, not months. Mr Ullah has no interest in overselling: you receive the same measured advice he gives every patient.

Augmentation in the cancer setting

Augmentation is also a reconstructive tool. After breast-conserving surgery or reconstruction, an implant on one or both sides can restore volume and symmetry. Patients with a personal or family history of breast cancer receive specific advice on imaging and follow-up with implants in place.

The operation and aftercare

The procedure takes around an hour under general anaesthetic, usually as a day case. Expect to be back at desk work within about a week and exercising fully at four to six weeks. Implants are not lifetime devices: most patients should expect further surgery at some point, and Mr Ullah explains the realistic long-term picture — including rare implant-associated conditions — before you decide.

This page is general information, not medical advice. Whether a procedure is right for you can only be decided in consultation, after proper assessment.

FAQ

Common questions

How long do breast implants last?

Implants do not need replacing on a fixed schedule, but neither do they last forever — many patients have revision surgery within 10–15 years for wear, capsular contracture or a change of preference. Plan for maintenance over a lifetime, not a one-off operation.

Are breast implants safe?

Modern implants are well studied and serious complications are rare, but not zero — including capsular contracture, rupture and BIA-ALCL, a rare lymphoma associated with certain textured implants. Mr Ullah covers the current evidence so your decision is genuinely informed.

Can I still have mammograms with implants?

Yes. Screening remains effective, though implants can obscure some breast tissue and radiographers use additional views. Always tell the screening service you have implants when booking.
