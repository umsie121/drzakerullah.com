**In short:** an inverted nipple you have had since your teens is common and usually harmless. A nipple that has newly turned inwards, especially on one side, should be checked by a doctor. Most new changes are benign, often age-related widening of the milk ducts, but a specialist will examine you and arrange imaging to be sure.

An inverted nipple is one that points inwards, or sits flat or sunken, rather than standing out. In clinic I see two quite different groups of people with the same description. Some have had inverted nipples for as long as they can remember and want to know if that is normal. Others have noticed a change, sometimes only in one breast, and are understandably worried.

The difference between those two situations matters more than anything else in this post. So that is where I will start.

## Lifelong or new: the question that matters most

**A lifelong inverted nipple** is usually present from puberty, when the breasts develop. It is often on both sides, although it can be on one. It happens because the milk ducts behind the nipple are a little short, or the tissue there is tight, and the nipple is held inwards. It is a normal variation, and it is not a sign of cancer.

**A newly inverted nipple** is a change in something that used to be different. Cancer Research UK lists a nipple that turns in or sinks into the breast among the changes to see a GP about. That is not because it usually means cancer. It is because the cause has to be found.

If you are not sure which group you are in, old photos can help, or ask someone who would know. If you still cannot tell, treat it as new and get it checked.

## How surgeons describe an inverted nipple

You may hear inverted nipples described by grade. This is simply a way of describing how easily the nipple comes out.

- **Grade 1.** The nipple can be drawn out easily with gentle pressure around the areola (the darker skin around the nipple), and stays out for a while. Some people find it comes out with cold or touch.
- **Grade 2.** The nipple can be drawn out, but goes back in quickly.
- **Grade 3.** The nipple is firmly held in and cannot be drawn out.

The grade helps when talking about breastfeeding or correction. It does not, on its own, tell me whether a new change is benign.

## Why a nipple may turn inwards later in life

When a nipple turns in for the first time in adult life, these are the causes I think about:

- **Duct ectasia.** As we get older, the milk ducts behind the nipple can widen and shorten. This is a common, benign change around the menopause. It often pulls the centre of the nipple in, giving a slit-like or horizontal appearance, and can come with a thick, creamy or greenish discharge. It is not linked to cancer.
- **Periductal mastitis.** Inflammation around the ducts, seen more often in younger women and strongly linked with smoking. It can cause pain, redness, discharge or an abscess near the areola, and the nipple may be pulled in.
- **Scarring.** Previous breast surgery, an old infection or an injury can tether the nipple.
- **Breastfeeding and weight change.** These can alter the shape of the breast and nipple over time, usually on both sides.
- **Breast cancer.** A cancer behind or near the nipple can pull the nipple in, often on one side only. It may come with a lump, skin dimpling, a change in breast shape, bloodstained discharge or a scaly rash on the nipple.

The last one is why I never assume. Most people I see with a new inverted nipple have a benign cause, but I want the tests to tell us that, not a guess.

Nipple changes can happen in men too. A newly inverted nipple in a man should always be checked, and I have written about [a lump behind the nipple in men](~/news/lump-behind-nipple-men-gynaecomastia/) separately.

## What happens when you are checked

The standard way of assessing any new breast symptom in the UK is triple assessment: examination, imaging and, if needed, a needle biopsy. It is how I approach every new symptom in my [breast clinic](~/procedures/benign-breast-conditions/).

1. **We talk.** When did you notice it? Is it one side or both? Can the nipple be drawn out? Any discharge, pain, redness or lump? I also ask about smoking, previous surgery and family history.
2. **I examine both breasts and both armpits.** A chaperone is always offered.
3. **Imaging.** Depending on your age, that is a mammogram (a low dose breast X-ray), an ultrasound scan, or both. Ultrasound is particularly good at showing widened ducts behind the nipple.
4. **Biopsy, if needed.** If the imaging shows anything that needs sampling, a small piece of tissue is taken with a needle under local anaesthetic.

If the inversion is from duct ectasia and the tests are reassuring, most people need no treatment at all. If there is troublesome discharge or recurrent infection, surgery to remove the ducts behind the nipple can help. If you also have discharge, my post on [nipple discharge causes](~/news/nipple-discharge-causes/) may be useful.

:::note
See your GP or a breast specialist promptly if a nipple has newly turned in, particularly on one side, or if it comes with a lump, skin dimpling, bloodstained discharge or a rash on the nipple. Most causes are benign, but they should be checked rather than watched.
:::

## Living with a lifelong inverted nipple

If you have always had inverted nipples and nothing has changed, you do not need tests just for that. Keep the area clean and dry, as skin can sometimes become sore where the nipple folds in. Carry on being breast aware, and attend NHS breast screening when you are invited.

**Breastfeeding.** Many women with inverted nipples breastfeed successfully, because a baby latches on to the areola and breast tissue, not just the nipple. Grade 3 nipples can make it harder. If you are expecting a baby, let your midwife know early, so you can be shown positioning and attachment techniques with an infant feeding specialist.

**Correction.** Some people simply prefer the appearance of nipples that stand out. Gentle suction devices can draw out grade 1 and some grade 2 nipples over time. Surgical correction is also possible. Some techniques release or divide the short ducts, which can affect the ability to breastfeed from that side, so timing matters if you may want children. Correction for appearance is usually not available on the NHS and is not normally covered by insurance. If you are considering it, we can talk about whether it suits you and how it would be arranged.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an inverted nipple a sign of breast cancer?
A lifelong inverted nipple is not. A nipple that has newly turned inwards can occasionally be caused by cancer, so it should be checked, although most new cases are benign.

### Why has my nipple suddenly gone inwards?
The most common cause in midlife is duct ectasia, a benign widening and shortening of the ducts behind the nipple. Inflammation, scarring and, less often, cancer can also cause it, which is why it needs examination and imaging.

### Can I breastfeed with inverted nipples?
Many women can, because a baby feeds from the areola and breast, not just the nipple. Tell your midwife early so you can get support with positioning and attachment.

### Can inverted nipples be fixed?
Yes. Suction devices help milder cases, and surgery can correct more firmly inverted nipples. Some operations can affect breastfeeding, so it is worth discussing timing before you decide.
