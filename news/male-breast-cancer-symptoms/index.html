Male breast cancer: the symptoms men should know

Male breast cancer symptoms explained by a consultant breast surgeon: the lump, nipple and skin changes to look for, who is at risk and how it is diagnosed.

By Mr Md Zaker Ullah FRCS, Consultant Oncoplastic Breast Surgeon.

In short: the commonest male breast cancer symptom is a firm, usually painless lump in the chest, often just to one side of the nipple. Other signs are a nipple that turns inwards, discharge (sometimes bloodstained), a sore or rash on the nipple, and a lump in the armpit. Most chest lumps in men are benign, but any of these should be checked promptly.

Men have breast tissue too. There is less of it than in women, but it can develop the same diseases, including cancer. Cancer Research UK puts the number of men diagnosed in the UK at around 420 a year. That is small next to the number of women, which is exactly why men tend not to think of it, and why some wait months before mentioning a change.

I have written separately about the lump behind the nipple that turns out to be gynaecomastia, which is by far the most common thing I see in men. This post is about the less common, more serious possibility: what male breast cancer looks and feels like, who is more likely to get it, and what happens if it is found.

The male breast cancer symptoms to look for

Because men have only a thin layer of breast tissue under the nipple, a cancer does not have far to go before it reaches the skin or the nipple itself. So the signs are often visible as well as something you can feel.

  • A lump in the chest. Typically hard, painless and fixed, so it does not slide about under the skin. It is often off-centre from the nipple rather than sitting directly beneath it.
  • A nipple that has newly pulled inwards. A nipple that has always been inverted is not a concern. One that has changed recently is.
  • Discharge from one nipple, especially if it is bloodstained or happens without squeezing.
  • A sore, crust, ulcer or rash on or around the nipple that does not heal over a few weeks. It can look a little like eczema.
  • Skin changes on the chest, such as puckering, dimpling, redness or swelling.
  • A lump or swelling in the armpit, which may be a lymph gland.
  • A change in the size or shape of one side of the chest compared with the other.

Pain is not a reliable guide either way. Many cancers are painless, and many benign lumps are tender. I would rather see a man with a painless lump than have him wait because it does not hurt.

How it differs from gynaecomastia

Gynaecomastia is benign enlargement of the breast gland. It usually feels like a soft or rubbery disc right behind the nipple, often on both sides, and it may be tender in its early months. Cancer is more often a hard lump on one side, slightly away from the nipple, sometimes with nipple or skin changes.

Those are tendencies, not rules. I cannot always tell the two apart by examination alone, and nor can anyone else. That is why every man with a new lump in the chest is offered a scan, and a biopsy if there is any doubt. If the scan confirms gynaecomastia, treatment options are covered on my gynaecomastia page.

Who is more likely to get it

Most men who develop breast cancer have no obvious risk factor. Even so, some things make it more likely.

  • Age. It is mainly a disease of older men, most often diagnosed over the age of 60. It can occasionally affect younger men.
  • An inherited gene variant. A faulty BRCA2 gene raises the risk in men more than any other known gene. BRCA1 and some other genes are also linked.
  • Family history. Close relatives with breast cancer (on either side of the family), or ovarian, prostate or pancreatic cancer, can point to an inherited variant.
  • Klinefelter syndrome. A genetic condition in which a man is born with an extra X chromosome. It is associated with higher oestrogen levels and a clearly increased risk.
  • Higher oestrogen levels from other causes, such as obesity, long-term liver disease or certain medicines.
  • Previous radiation to the chest, for example radiotherapy for lymphoma when younger.

If you know your family carries a BRCA2 variant, it is worth being particularly alert to chest changes and talking to your GP or a genetics service about what it means for you.

Why men delay, and why it matters

In clinic I hear the same reasons again and again. "I didn't know men could get it." "I thought it was a cyst." "I was embarrassed." Some men notice a change and only come in when their partner insists.

Because there is so little tissue between the nipple and the chest muscle, a cancer in a man can reach the skin or the lymph glands under the arm sooner than it might in a woman. Coming forward early gives the best chance of treating it at an early stage, with a smaller operation and fewer additional treatments.

There is nothing unusual about a man attending a breast clinic, and the staff are used to it. The examination is short, you can bring someone with you, and a chaperone is always offered.

If you have noticed any of these changes, see your GP or a breast specialist without waiting to see whether it settles. In the NHS, NICE guideline NG12 sets out when a GP should make an urgent suspected cancer referral, and an unexplained breast lump is one of those reasons, for men as well as women. Privately, you can book an assessment directly at The Holly in Buckhurst Hill, Canary Wharf or St Bartholomew's.

How male breast cancer is diagnosed

Men are assessed in the same way as women, using what we call triple assessment.

  1. Examination of both sides of the chest and both armpits by a breast specialist.
  2. Imaging. Usually an ultrasound of the chest and armpit, and often a mammogram as well. Mammograms are perfectly possible in men, although there is less tissue to compress.
  3. A needle biopsy of anything uncertain. This is a core biopsy under local anaesthetic, which takes a few small samples of tissue for the laboratory.

At my Tuesday afternoon Rapid Access Breast Clinic at Nuffield Health The Holly, the examination and scan happen at the same visit, and a biopsy can be taken there and then if needed. I can usually explain what the scan shows before you leave. If a biopsy is taken, I will tell you when to expect the result and how you will hear it.

If cancer is confirmed, further scans may be needed to check whether it has spread, and every case is discussed by a multidisciplinary team of surgeons, radiologists, pathologists and oncologists before treatment is agreed.

What treatment usually involves

Treatment is planned around the type and stage of the cancer, so I will only give a broad outline here.

  • Surgery is usually the first step. Most men have a mastectomy, removing the breast tissue and usually the nipple. Because the male breast is small, keeping the nipple is rarely practical, and the resulting scar is generally flat and straightforward to look after.
  • Lymph node surgery. A sentinel lymph node biopsy samples the first one or few glands in the armpit. If cancer has spread to the glands, more may need to be removed.
  • Hormone therapy. Most male breast cancers are driven by hormones, so a tablet such as tamoxifen is commonly recommended, usually for at least five years.
  • Radiotherapy, chemotherapy or targeted drugs are added for some men, depending on the features of the cancer.
  • Genetic testing. Men with breast cancer are usually offered a referral for genetic testing, because the result can matter for their children, brothers and sisters.

More detail on the operations is on my breast cancer surgery page. If you have already been given a diagnosis and a plan elsewhere and want it looked at again, I am always happy to offer a second opinion.

Questions

What does a male breast cancer lump feel like?

Usually hard, painless and fixed in place, often to one side of the nipple. Some are smaller or softer than that, so any new lump should be examined and scanned.

Is breast cancer in men common?

No. Cancer Research UK estimates around 420 men are diagnosed in the UK each year, compared with many thousands of women. Most chest lumps in men turn out to be gynaecomastia.

Can young men get breast cancer?

It is very uncommon under 40, and most men diagnosed are over 60. Younger men with a strong family history or a known BRCA2 variant should still have any change checked.

Do men get mammograms?

Yes, when they have symptoms. There is no screening programme for men, but a mammogram and ultrasound are a standard part of assessing a lump in the male chest.

This article is general information and not medical advice. If you have a breast symptom, arrange an assessment through your GP or a specialist clinic. In an emergency, call 999.
