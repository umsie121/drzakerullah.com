Breast cancer surgery, planned twice

Surgery is usually the cornerstone of breast cancer treatment. Mr Ullah's oncoplastic training means every operation is planned twice over: once to remove the cancer completely, and once for the breast — and the person — that remains.

Breast-conserving surgery

Where the tumour and breast size allow, the preferred operation is usually a wide local excision (lumpectomy) — removing the cancer with a margin of healthy tissue while keeping the breast. Oncoplastic techniques allow Mr Ullah to remove larger areas than conventional surgery would permit, then reshape the remaining tissue so the breast keeps a natural form.

In women with larger breasts this can take the form of a therapeutic mammoplasty — removing the cancer using breast reduction techniques, so the patient leaves with the cancer treated and a lighter, lifted breast. The other breast can be matched at the same time or later.

Mastectomy

Some cancers — and some patients' preferences — are better served by mastectomy. Where appropriate, this can be a skin-sparing or nipple-sparing operation with immediate reconstruction, so you wake from surgery with the breast shape already restored. Mr Ullah discusses reconstruction before mastectomy, never after.

The lymph nodes

Staging the armpit (axilla) is part of almost every breast cancer operation. For most patients this means a sentinel lymph node biopsy — removing only the first one to three nodes that drain the breast, identified with a tracer — rather than clearing the whole axilla. This gives your oncologists the information they need while greatly reducing the risk of arm swelling (lymphoedema).

A team decision

Every cancer case is reviewed by a multidisciplinary team — radiologists, pathologists, oncologists and specialist nurses — before and after surgery, in line with UK best practice. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy and hormone therapy are coordinated around the operation so that each part of treatment supports the others.

FAQ

Common questions

How soon after diagnosis will I have surgery?

Once the multidisciplinary team has confirmed the plan, private surgery can usually be scheduled promptly — commonly within one to two weeks, depending on the operation and any tests still needed. Some patients have chemotherapy before surgery; if so, that is a deliberate part of the plan, not a delay.

Will I lose my breast?

Most breast cancers today are treated with breast-conserving surgery, and oncoplastic techniques have widened who is eligible. Where mastectomy is the safer choice, immediate reconstruction is usually possible. The decision is always made with you, with every option on the table.

What is oncoplastic breast surgery?

Oncoplastic surgery combines complete removal of the cancer with plastic surgical techniques to preserve or restore the shape of the breast, in the same operation. Evidence shows cancer control is at least as good as standard surgery. Read Mr Ullah's guide.

What are the risks of breast cancer surgery?

As with any operation there are risks of bleeding, infection, seroma (fluid collection) and scarring, plus procedure-specific risks such as changes in sensation or, after axillary surgery, lymphoedema. Mr Ullah goes through the risks relevant to your operation in detail before you consent.

Can I have a second opinion before surgery?

Yes — and it is your right. Many patients see Mr Ullah for a second opinion on a diagnosis or a proposed operation. Bring any imaging, biopsy results and clinic letters. More on second opinions.
