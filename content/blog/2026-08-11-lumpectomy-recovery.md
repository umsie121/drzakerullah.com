**In short:** lumpectomy recovery is usually quicker than people expect. Most women go home the same day, need simple painkillers for a week or so, and return to desk work within one to two weeks. Wounds heal over two to three weeks. If lymph nodes were removed from the armpit, arm exercises matter, and radiotherapy usually starts a few weeks later.

The decision about which operation to have is a separate conversation, and I have written about it in [lumpectomy or mastectomy](~/news/lumpectomy-vs-mastectomy/). This post is about what comes after the decision, once the date is booked. The questions I hear in clinic are nearly always practical: when can I shower, which bra, when can I drive, when will I know the results. I have answered them roughly in the order they come up.

Everyone heals at their own pace, and your operation may differ from the typical one described here. Your own team's written instructions always come first.

## The day of surgery and the first night

A lumpectomy, also called a wide local excision or [breast conserving surgery](~/procedures/breast-cancer-surgery/), is usually done under general anaesthetic and is very often a day case. Most of my patients are home by the evening, or the next morning if the operation was later in the day or included more extensive surgery to the armpit.

You will need someone to drive you home and, ideally, to stay with you that first night. The breast will feel sore and tight rather than sharply painful for most people. Paracetamol, sometimes with an anti-inflammatory, is usually enough. Take it regularly for the first few days rather than waiting for the discomfort to build.

Numbness around the scar and in the inner upper arm (after armpit surgery) is common and often improves over months, though some of it can be permanent.

## Lumpectomy recovery in week one: wounds, bras and rest

**Wounds.** The skin is usually closed with dissolvable stitches under the surface, so there is nothing to remove. The wound is covered with a waterproof dressing or glue. Most people can shower from the day after surgery, keeping the water pressure gentle on the breast and patting the area dry. Avoid soaking in a bath or swimming until the wound has fully healed.

**Bra.** A soft, supportive, non-wired bra worn day and night for the first week or two helps a great deal. It holds the breast still and eases the ache. A sports bra that fastens at the front is often easiest. Bring it to hospital so you can put it on before going home.

**Drains.** After a lumpectomy, a drain (a thin tube that removes fluid from the wound) is now rarely needed. They are used more often after a full clearance of the armpit lymph nodes or larger oncoplastic operations.

**Bruising and swelling.** The breast may look bruised and slightly larger for a couple of weeks, which settles on its own. If you had a tracer injection for a sentinel node biopsy, you may notice a blue tinge to the skin or blue-green urine for a day or two if a blue dye was used. Both are harmless.

**Rest, but keep moving.** Short walks from the first day lower the chance of a blood clot or chest infection. Avoid lifting anything heavy, such as a full shopping bag or a toddler, for the first week or so.

## Seroma: the swelling that worries people most

A seroma is a collection of clear fluid under the wound, most common in the armpit after lymph node surgery. It feels like a soft, squashy swelling, sometimes with a sense of sloshing, and tends to appear in the first week or two.

A seroma is not a sign that anything has gone wrong. Most are absorbed by the body over a few weeks. If one becomes tight or uncomfortable, your breast care nurse or I can drain it with a fine needle in clinic, which takes a few minutes. Small ones are best left alone, as they often refill after drainage.

:::note
Contact your breast team promptly if the wound becomes red, hot or increasingly painful, if it leaks pus or cloudy fluid, if the breast swells quickly and becomes tense, or if you develop a fever or feel generally unwell. These can be signs of infection or bleeding, which are uncommon and treatable.
:::

## Arm exercises after armpit surgery

Almost every breast cancer operation includes checking the lymph nodes in the armpit (the axilla). For most women this is a sentinel node biopsy, where only the first one to three nodes are removed. Some need more nodes taken.

After any armpit surgery the shoulder can stiffen, and a tight cord of tissue running down the inner arm (cording) sometimes develops a few weeks later. Gentle, regular exercises help prevent much of this. Your team will give you a set to follow, usually starting the day after surgery with simple movements and building up to lifting the arm above your head once the wound allows. Breast Cancer Now publishes a clear [exercise guide after breast surgery](https://breastcancernow.org/about-breast-cancer/life-after-treatment/your-body-after-breast-cancer-treatment/exercises-after-breast-cancer-surgery), which is worth printing out.

Lymphoedema, a long-term swelling of the arm, is much less likely after a sentinel node biopsy than after a full clearance, but it is worth knowing the early signs: a feeling of heaviness, rings or sleeves becoming tight. Mention it early if you notice it.

## Weeks two to four: results, and sometimes a second operation

**Your results appointment.** The tissue removed is examined in detail by a pathologist, and the findings are discussed at the multidisciplinary team meeting. You will usually come back about two weeks after surgery to go through them. This is where you learn the final size and type of the cancer, whether the lymph nodes were clear and whether the margins were clear.

**Margins and re-excision.** A clear margin means a rim of healthy tissue all round the cancer. In a minority of women, cancer cells or precancerous change (DCIS) reach the edge of the tissue removed. National guidance from NICE recommends further surgery in that situation. This is usually a short second operation, called a re-excision, to remove a little more tissue from that edge, and it is normally another day case. Occasionally a mastectomy is the better option, and we would discuss that carefully.

Needing a re-excision does not mean the cancer has spread or that the first operation was done badly. It reflects the fact that some cancers, and DCIS in particular, cannot be fully seen on scans.

**Driving.** You can drive when you can wear a seatbelt comfortably, turn to look over your shoulder and make an emergency stop without hesitating. For many people that is somewhere between one and two weeks, sometimes longer after armpit surgery. Check your car insurance policy too.

**Work.** Most people return to desk or home-based work within one to two weeks. A physical job, or one involving lifting, may need three to four weeks or more. Your team will advise on what suits your job.

## Weeks four to six: exercise, and starting radiotherapy

By four weeks most women feel close to normal day to day, although tiredness can linger.

**Exercise.** Walking can continue throughout. Gentle swimming is usually fine once the wound has completely healed. Running, the gym, yoga and heavier upper-body work are typically reintroduced gradually from around four to six weeks. Let comfort guide you, and ask your team if you are unsure.

**Radiotherapy.** After breast conserving surgery, radiotherapy to the breast is usual, because together the two treatments give the same long-term survival as mastectomy for most early breast cancers. It normally starts a few weeks after surgery, once the wound has healed, and is often a short daily course over one to three weeks. If you need chemotherapy, radiotherapy usually comes after it. The oncologist plans this with you, and your results appointment is a good time to ask when it is likely to begin.

**The scar and the shape.** The scar stays pink and firm for some months before it softens, and the breast may feel lumpy where it has healed inside. That is normal, but ask about any new lump. If the breast is left with a dent, [fat grafting](~/procedures/fat-grafting/) can often improve it later, once radiotherapy changes have settled.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to recover from a lumpectomy?
Most women feel largely back to normal within two to three weeks, and return to desk work within one to two. Recovery can take longer after armpit surgery, and tiredness may last a little longer.

### How long should I wear a bra after a lumpectomy?
A soft, supportive, non-wired bra day and night is usually advised for at least the first week or two. Many women find it comfortable to carry on for longer, particularly during radiotherapy.

### Is it normal to have a lump after a lumpectomy?
Some firmness under the scar is very common as the tissue heals, and a soft swelling can be a seroma. Any lump that is growing, painful or new should be checked by your breast team.

### Will I need another operation after a lumpectomy?
Most women do not. A minority need a re-excision if the margins are not clear, and your surgeon will tell you at the results appointment.
