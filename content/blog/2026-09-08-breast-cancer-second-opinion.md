**In short:** a breast cancer second opinion is worth considering when a mastectomy has been advised and you want to know if your breast can be kept, when reconstruction has not been fully discussed, or when the diagnosis feels unclear. It rarely needs to delay treatment. Bring your reports and imaging, and expect your current team to be supportive.

Patients who ask me for a second opinion often apologise for doing so. There is no need. Most of the time they are not unhappy with their team. They have been given life-changing news, a plan has been made quickly, and they want to be sure it is the right plan for them before signing a consent form. That is a reasonable thing to want.

I see second opinions in both directions. Sometimes I confirm exactly what has already been recommended, and the patient goes back to their own hospital reassured. Sometimes there is another option worth considering. Both outcomes are useful.

## Do you have a right to a second opinion?

In the NHS there is no legal right to a second opinion, but you are entitled to ask for one, and it is very rarely refused. The GMC expects doctors to respect a patient's wish to seek another view. Cancer Research UK has a clear page on [how to get a second opinion](https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/treatment/access-to-treatment/different-doctor-second-opinion), including through the NHS.

Within the NHS, the usual route is to ask your consultant or your GP to refer you to another breast unit. You cannot always choose a particular surgeon, and it can take a little time to arrange.

Privately, you can usually be seen within days. Many insurers cover a second opinion consultation, although you should check with yours and get a pre-authorisation number first. I am a Bupa Platinum consultant, fee-assured with Bupa, and I am part of the Bupa Open Referral network. If you are self-funding, the fee is confirmed when you book. The practice can explain how this works before you book.

A second opinion does not usually mean you move your care. Most people return to their own team with a letter setting out what I found and what I would recommend. If you would prefer to have your treatment with me, that is your choice, and it can be arranged.

## When a breast cancer second opinion is most worth having

Some situations come up again and again in my clinic.

**A mastectomy has been recommended.** For many early breast cancers, breast conserving surgery with radiotherapy gives the same long-term survival as mastectomy. Whether it is possible depends on the size and position of the cancer relative to the breast. [Oncoplastic techniques](~/news/what-is-oncoplastic-breast-surgery/), which reshape the breast during the cancer operation, can make breast conservation possible for some women who might otherwise have been offered mastectomy. Sometimes mastectomy is still the right advice, and I will say so plainly. I have written more about [how the lumpectomy or mastectomy decision is made](~/news/lumpectomy-vs-mastectomy/).

**Reconstruction has not been discussed in detail.** If you need a mastectomy, you should know whether [immediate breast reconstruction](~/procedures/breast-reconstruction/) is an option before the operation, not after it. The choice of technique depends on whether radiotherapy is likely, your build, your general health and your own preferences. Some women decide against reconstruction, and that is a valid decision too. What matters is that the choice is made with full information.

**The pathology or imaging is unclear.** Occasionally a biopsy result does not quite match what the scans show, or the extent of the disease is uncertain. A second look at the images, or a request for the slides to be reviewed by another specialist pathologist, can clarify things.

**You carry a BRCA or other gene variant.** Decisions about risk-reducing surgery are complex and very personal, and there is usually time to think them through.

**You want to talk it through in your first language.** I consult in English and Bengali, and some families find a diagnosis much easier to discuss that way.

## Will a second opinion delay my treatment?

For most breast cancers, a short pause of a week or two to be certain of the plan does not affect the outcome. Breast cancer is usually treated within weeks, and the extra time is small compared with the importance of the decision.

That said, a second opinion should be quick and focused. Arrange it promptly, keep your existing appointments in the meantime, and do not cancel anything at your current hospital until you have decided. If your cancer is one where timing really matters, for example a fast-growing cancer where chemotherapy before surgery has been planned, I will tell you directly and help you avoid any delay.

## What to bring

The more information I have, the more useful the opinion. Please bring, or send ahead:

- clinic letters from your breast team, including the multidisciplinary team (MDT) outcome if you have it
- imaging reports, and the images themselves if possible (mammogram, ultrasound and any MRI)
- biopsy and pathology reports, including receptor status (ER, PR and HER2)
- any genetic test results
- a list of your medicines and other health conditions
- the questions you most want answered, written down

You can ask your hospital for copies of your records, and the private secretary can help request them. If some results are still awaited, it is often better to wait for them so that the second opinion is complete.

## How MDTs work, and why that matters

In the UK, every breast cancer case is discussed by a multidisciplinary team: surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists and breast care nurses. This is standard practice across NHS and private cancer care, and it is how I work in both. The MDT recommendation is a careful, collective view, not one doctor's opinion.

So a second opinion is not about overruling a team. It is about looking at the same facts with you in the room, with time to explain the reasoning and to consider what you value most. Often the medical facts point to one clear answer. Sometimes there are two or three sound options, and the right one depends on you. When my view differs, I write it down clearly so that your team can consider it alongside their own.

Much of a second opinion is a review of reports and images, so it can often be done by video or telephone consultation. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I get a second opinion on breast cancer on the NHS?
Yes, you can ask your consultant or GP to refer you to another NHS breast team. There is no legal right to one, but it is very rarely refused.

### Will my surgeon be offended if I ask for a second opinion?
Most consultants welcome it. It is a normal part of good care, and your team can share your scans and reports to make the review complete.

### How quickly can I get a private breast cancer second opinion?
A private second opinion can usually be arranged within days, and a video consultation often within 24 to 48 hours.

### Does a second opinion mean I have to change hospitals?
No. Most patients take the opinion back to their own team. You can choose to move your care, but you do not have to.
