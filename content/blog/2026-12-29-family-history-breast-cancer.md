**In short:** most people with a family history of breast cancer are at only slightly or moderately raised risk, and do not need genetic testing. Your family history breast cancer risk rises if relatives were diagnosed young, if several are affected on one side, or if there is ovarian or male breast cancer. Discuss those patterns with your GP.

Questions about family history come up in my clinics every week. Sometimes it is mentioned in passing during a visit about a lump. Sometimes it is the whole reason for the visit: a sister has just been diagnosed, and the family wants to know what it means for them.

Breast cancer is common, so many families have at least one person who has had it. In most of those families it has happened by chance, not because of a single faulty gene. The aim of an assessment is to find the minority of families where genes play a large part, and to reassure everyone else.

## What counts as a family history of breast cancer

Doctors talk about relatives by "degree", because that is how genes are shared.

- **First-degree relatives** are your parents, brothers, sisters and children.
- **Second-degree relatives** are your grandparents, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, half-brothers and half-sisters.

Your father's side counts just as much as your mother's. A faulty gene can be passed down through a man who never develops cancer himself, so a strong history among your father's sisters or his mother matters too.

The details that help most are simple: who had cancer, which type, their age at diagnosis, and whether it affected one breast or both. NICE also advises asking about Jewish ancestry, because some BRCA gene changes are more common in people with Ashkenazi Jewish heritage. If you can, talk to relatives before your appointment.

## How family history changes breast cancer risk

NICE, which sets the standards for the NHS in England, places people into broad groups rather than giving everyone a precise number.

- **Near population risk.** For example, one relative diagnosed with breast cancer over the age of 40, with nothing else unusual in the family. Your risk is close to that of any other woman your age. NICE advises that you can be looked after by your GP, with standard advice on breast awareness and screening.
- **Moderate risk.** A family pattern that raises your risk above average but does not suggest a strong inherited gene. Women in this group may be offered extra mammograms from their forties.
- **High risk.** A pattern that suggests an inherited gene may be involved, or a known faulty gene in the family. These women are usually seen in a specialist genetics clinic and may be offered more intensive screening, genetic testing and a discussion of ways to reduce risk.

## When NICE advises a referral

NICE guidance on familial breast cancer (CG164) sets out when a GP should offer referral to a breast or family history clinic. For people who have not had breast cancer themselves, the main patterns are:

- one mother, sister or daughter diagnosed with breast cancer under the age of 40
- one father, brother or son diagnosed with breast cancer at any age
- one first-degree relative with cancer in both breasts, where the first was diagnosed under the age of 50
- two first-degree relatives, or one first-degree and one second-degree relative, diagnosed with breast cancer at any age
- one relative with breast cancer and one with ovarian cancer, at any age, where at least one is a first-degree relative
- three first-degree or second-degree relatives diagnosed with breast cancer at any age

NICE also asks GPs to seek advice if the family includes Jewish ancestry, a sarcoma (a cancer of bone or soft tissue) in a relative under 45, or an unusual pattern of several cancers at young ages. If a faulty gene such as BRCA1, BRCA2 or TP53 has already been found in a relative, you can be referred straight to a genetics service.

These are guides, not hard rules. If your GP is unsure, they can ask the local breast or genetics team for advice.

## When to ask about genetic testing

Genetic testing looks for changes in genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 that greatly raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer. It is most useful when there is a real chance of finding something.

In the NHS, specialists use family history tools, such as CanRisk or the Manchester score, to estimate the chance that a family carries a faulty gene. NICE recommends offering testing when that chance is around 10% or more. Eligibility criteria are reviewed from time to time, so the genetics team will apply the current rules to your own family.

A few points that I find patients are often not told:

1. **The best person to test first is usually a relative who has had cancer.** If a faulty gene is found in them, other family members can then have a simple, targeted test. Testing someone who has never had cancer first is less informative, because a normal result does not rule out a gene that has not yet been identified in the family.
2. **Counselling comes before the test.** NICE advises that predictive testing should not be offered without proper genetic counselling. It covers what a positive or negative result would mean for you and your family.
3. **Not every result is clear.** Sometimes the test finds a "variant of uncertain significance", a gene change whose effect is not yet known. That is not the same as a positive result, and it should not on its own lead to surgery.
4. **There is a separate NHS route for Jewish ancestry.** In England, people with at least one Jewish grandparent can ask about BRCA testing through a dedicated NHS programme, even without a family history of cancer.

Private genetic tests are available, but any result should be interpreted by someone with genetics training.

## What happens if your risk is raised

For most women at moderate risk, the plan is straightforward: being breast aware, a yearly mammogram from the age of 40, and joining the routine NHS screening programme later. Women at higher risk may also be offered yearly MRI scans, usually from their thirties. NICE also recommends that some women at raised risk are offered medicines that lower the chance of breast cancer developing, and this is discussed with the genetics or breast team.

For women who carry a BRCA or other high-risk gene change, risk-reducing surgery is one option among several. This might mean removing both breasts, often with [breast reconstruction](~/procedures/breast-reconstruction/) at the same operation, and for some women removal of the ovaries, which a gynaecologist would discuss. It is a large decision, never an urgent one, and screening alone is an equally valid choice for many women.

If you have already been diagnosed with breast cancer and have a strong family history, genetic results can sometimes affect the choice of operation. I have written more about that in [lumpectomy or mastectomy, and how the decision is made](~/news/lumpectomy-vs-mastectomy/), and the options are set out on my [breast cancer surgery page](~/procedures/breast-cancer-surgery/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does my mother's breast cancer mean I will get it?
No. Most women whose mother had breast cancer never develop it themselves. Your risk depends on how old she was at diagnosis and on the rest of the family history, so it is worth discussing with your GP.

### Can I get BRCA testing on the NHS?
Yes, if your personal or family history meets the current NHS criteria, or if a faulty gene has already been found in a relative. The genetics team uses family history tools to decide, and testing usually starts with a relative who has had cancer.

### Does breast cancer on my father's side count?
Yes. Genes are passed down equally through both parents, so breast or ovarian cancer among your father's relatives counts just as much as on your mother's side.

### At what age should I start screening with a family history?
That depends on your risk group. Women at moderate risk are usually offered yearly mammograms from 40, and women at high risk may start earlier, sometimes with MRI. Your GP or specialist will advise on your own plan.
