Breast cancer

Five things every woman should know about breast cancer

A consultant breast surgeon's plain-English guide to breast cancer risk, the symptoms that matter, screening, and why outcomes are better than most fear.

Mr Zaker Ullah FRCS16 July 2026 · 3 min read · As featured in West Essex Life
Contents
1. It is common, and survivable2. Early detection changes everything3. Know the symptoms beyond "a lump"4. Some risk is within your control5. Treatment today is personalQuestions

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in the UK, and it is also one of the most treatable. A version of this article first appeared in West Essex Life magazine. After more than three decades in surgery, these are the five points I find myself repeating most often in clinic.

1. It is common, and survivable

Around one in seven UK women will develop breast cancer in their lifetime. That number frightens people, but it should be read alongside another: the great majority of women diagnosed today will survive their cancer. Outcomes have improved steadily for forty years, driven by earlier detection and better, more personalised treatment. A breast cancer diagnosis today is a serious event, not a sentence.

2. Early detection changes everything

The single biggest factor you can influence is how early a cancer is found. Small cancers that have not spread to the lymph nodes are usually cured with far less treatment, often breast-conserving surgery and a short course of radiotherapy. Attend NHS screening when invited: mammography finds cancers years before they can be felt. Between screens, know your own breasts well enough to notice change.

3. Know the symptoms beyond "a lump"

A new lump is the best-known symptom, but not the only one. See a doctor promptly about any of the following:

  • A lump or thickening in the breast or armpit
  • A change in the size or shape of one breast
  • Skin dimpling, puckering or an "orange-peel" texture
  • A newly inverted nipple, a nipple rash or discharge, especially if bloodstained
  • Persistent pain in one spot of the breast

Most of these turn out to be benign. All of them deserve assessment.

4. Some risk is within your control

Age and family history cannot be changed. But maintaining a healthy weight after the menopause, limiting alcohol and staying physically active each measurably reduce risk. If several close relatives have had breast or ovarian cancer, particularly at a young age, ask about a family history clinic. Genetic testing and enhanced screening exist for those who need them.

5. Treatment today is personal

Modern treatment is tailored to the biology of each tumour. Two women with "breast cancer" may have entirely different plans. One has surgery first, another chemotherapy first. One needs a mastectomy, another a small excision through a discreet scar. Oncoplastic surgery means that removing the cancer completely no longer requires accepting a damaged breast. Ask your team why your plan is right for your cancer; a good team will welcome the question. If you are unsure, a second opinion is always reasonable.

If you have a symptom, don't wait for it to become an emergency. A one-stop clinic can usually give you an answer, most often a reassuring one, in a single visit.

Questions

How common is breast cancer in the UK?

Around one in seven women in the UK will be diagnosed with breast cancer during their lifetime. It is the most common cancer in the UK, but survival has improved steadily for decades.

Does breast pain mean cancer?

Breast pain on its own is rarely a sign of cancer. Persistent pain in one specific spot should still be assessed, along with any other new change.
