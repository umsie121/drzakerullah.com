**In short:** a sentinel node biopsy removes the first one to three lymph nodes in the armpit that drain the breast, to check whether breast cancer has reached them. A tracer is injected beforehand so the nodes can be found. It is usually done during the main breast operation, through a small cut, and most people recover within one to two weeks.

When I explain a breast cancer operation in clinic, patients often ask why I need to operate on the armpit at all when the lump is in the breast, and whether it means the cancer has spread.

It usually does not mean that. Checking the lymph nodes is part of almost every breast cancer operation, because the result helps the team decide what treatment, if any, is needed after surgery.

## What is a sentinel node biopsy?

Lymph nodes, sometimes called glands, are small bean-shaped structures that filter fluid from the tissues. Fluid from the breast drains mainly to the nodes in the armpit (the axilla). If cancer cells escape from a breast cancer, the first place they usually travel to is the first node, or small group of nodes, in that chain. These are the sentinel nodes.

The idea of a sentinel node biopsy is simple. Rather than removing all the nodes in the armpit, I remove only these first one to three nodes and send them to the laboratory. If they are clear, the other nodes are very likely to be clear too, and they can stay where they are.

That matters because the lymph nodes do a job. Removing most of them, an operation called an axillary clearance, carries a higher risk of long-term arm swelling (lymphoedema). A sentinel node biopsy gives the oncologists the information they need while keeping that risk much lower. I describe how it fits with the rest of the operation on my [breast cancer surgery page](~/procedures/breast-cancer-surgery/).

## Who needs one, and who does not?

Before any operation, the armpit is checked with an ultrasound scan as part of your assessment. If a node looks normal, a sentinel node biopsy at the time of surgery is the usual next step.

If a node looks abnormal, a needle biopsy is taken first. When that shows cancer in the node, a sentinel node biopsy is not usually the right test, because we already know the answer. Instead the plan might be chemotherapy first, followed by a **targeted axillary dissection**, which removes the specific nodes that were affected, or an axillary clearance. These are planned by the multidisciplinary team, the radiologists, pathologists, oncologists and specialist nurses who meet to agree each patient's treatment.

Sentinel node biopsy is also used for some people with DCIS (ductal carcinoma in situ, an early non-invasive change), particularly when a mastectomy is planned, because it cannot easily be done afterwards. For a minority of patients with small, lower-risk cancers, the team may discuss whether the armpit needs surgery at all. That is a decision for your own case, not a general rule.

## What happens before and during the operation

The nodes are small and lie in fatty tissue, so the surgeon needs a way to find them. This is done with a tracer, a substance injected into the breast that travels along the lymph channels to the sentinel nodes. The type of tracer varies between hospitals, and I will tell you which one is used for you.

- **A radioactive tracer.** A very small amount is injected into the breast, usually on the morning of surgery or the day before, often in the hospital's nuclear medicine or imaging department. The dose of radiation is small. During the operation I use a handheld probe that detects the tracer and leads me to the right nodes.
- **A blue dye.** This is often used alongside the radioactive tracer and is injected once you are asleep. The nodes take up the dye and turn blue, which helps confirm I have the right ones.
- **A magnetic tracer.** Some hospitals use this instead. It is injected before surgery and found with a magnetic probe.

The biopsy itself is usually done during the same operation as the breast surgery, whether that is a [lumpectomy or a mastectomy](~/news/lumpectomy-vs-mastectomy/). It is done through a small separate cut in the armpit, often hidden in a skin crease, or sometimes through the mastectomy incision. It adds a modest amount of time to the operation, and many patients still go home the same day.

## The tracer: small things to know

- If blue dye is used, your urine may look blue or green for a day or two. This is harmless.
- The breast skin may have a blue tinge near the injection site. This fades, but it can take several weeks, occasionally longer.
- A small number of people have an allergic reaction to blue dye. The anaesthetic team watches for this and is prepared to treat it.
- A magnetic tracer can leave a brownish mark on the skin and can affect later MRI scans of that breast, so please mention it if an MRI is planned.

## Getting your sentinel node biopsy results

The nodes are examined in the laboratory along with the tissue from the breast. The results usually take one to two weeks and are then reviewed by the multidisciplinary team before I go through them with you.

There are broadly three possible findings:

1. **The nodes are clear.** This is the most common result. No further surgery to the armpit is needed.
2. **A very small amount of cancer is found.** Often this needs no further treatment to the armpit, though it is taken into account when planning other treatment.
3. **A larger amount of cancer is found.** The team may recommend further treatment to the armpit, either surgery to remove more nodes or radiotherapy to that area. The choice depends on your cancer and your overall plan.

If your results are not straightforward, please ask as many questions as you need. A [second opinion](~/second-opinion/) on the plan is always reasonable, and I am happy to give one.

## Recovery after a sentinel node biopsy

Most people find the armpit is sore and a little bruised for a week or two. It is usually less uncomfortable than patients expect. Simple pain relief is generally enough.

- **Arm and shoulder exercises.** The breast care team will give you gentle exercises, usually starting the day after surgery. They help prevent stiffness and are worth doing every day.
- **Numbness.** The skin of the upper inner arm or armpit may feel numb or tingly, because small nerves pass through the area. This often improves over months, though for some people a patch of altered feeling remains.
- **Fluid.** Fluid can collect under the wound (a seroma). It often settles by itself, and if it becomes uncomfortable it can be drained in clinic with a needle.
- **Cording.** A few people notice a tight cord running from the armpit down the inner arm, a few weeks after surgery. It usually eases with stretching and time.
- **Work and driving.** Most people return to desk work within one to two weeks. You can drive once you can turn the wheel and make an emergency stop comfortably. More detail is in my post on [recovery after a lumpectomy](~/news/lumpectomy-recovery/).

## Lymphoedema: the risk to keep in mind

Lymphoedema is swelling of the arm, hand or chest wall that happens when lymph fluid does not drain as well as it should. The risk is much lower after a sentinel node biopsy than after an axillary clearance, but it is not zero.

The early signs are a feeling of heaviness or fullness in the arm, rings or watch straps becoming tight, or a sleeve feeling snug on one side. If you notice any of these, tell your breast care nurse or me early, because treatment works better when it starts sooner. Cancer Research UK has clear information on [lymphoedema after breast cancer treatment](https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/breast-cancer/living-with/lymphoedema-after-treatment).

If you have a new breast lump or a change in your breast or armpit and have not yet been assessed, please see your GP or [book an appointment](~/booking/) promptly. Most breast symptoms are benign, but every new symptom deserves a proper assessment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a sentinel node biopsy mean my cancer has spread?
No. It is a routine check carried out for almost everyone having breast cancer surgery. In most patients the sentinel nodes are clear.

### Is the radioactive tracer safe?
Yes. The amount of radiation is very small. The hospital will give you any specific advice, for example if you are breastfeeding.

### How long does it take to recover from a sentinel node biopsy?
Most people feel comfortable within one to two weeks and return to desk work in that time. Shoulder exercises from the breast care team help you regain full movement.

### Will I need more surgery if cancer is found in the nodes?
Not always. A very small amount of cancer often needs no further treatment to the armpit. If more is found, the team may recommend further surgery or radiotherapy, and we discuss the options together.
